# 0113: Dates to the end of 2040 for every site (extends ADR 0104)

Status: accepted, 15 November 2026 (night); numbered 0113 after merging main, which already had a 0109. Adopted by the author after a competing entry was seen to cover 2027 to 2040 while this
tool stopped at the end of 2030.

## Context

ADR 0104 opened every listed site and own coordinates to the end of 2030: the frozen JPL Horizons tables up to 2027-11-01, the
in-browser engine (Astronomy Engine, `src/moonsky.js`) after them, on the same hourly grid and with the same horizon. The engine
label on the cards says "validated against JPL Horizons" only when `data/validation-years.json` passes the rule in
`config/years.json`. Nothing in the engine stops at 2030; the limit was the accepted range and the checked range.

## Decision

- `config/years.json` `stop` = 2041-01-01 00:00 UTC, so all of 31 December 2040, for every listed site and own coordinates. No code
  change: the range, the date fields, "Now" and the engine blocks (`chunk_days` 365, `pad_days` 45) all read the config.
- The check of the later years covers the whole engine range up to the new end: `check.from` 2028-01-01, `check.to` 2041-01-01,
  `check.pairs` 60 (was 24), same fixed seed 20261116, never on a table hour, a fresh one-minute JPL Horizons request per pair and
  body with the script's pause between requests (`config/validation.json` `pause_seconds`). Rule unchanged: every difference at most
  `max_diff_deg` (0.05°) and every yes/no answer the same, otherwise the cards say the later years are not validated.
- Tests: the accepted range ends 2041-01-01; the check reaches that end; the recorded run has at least 20 pairs in 2031 to 2040;
  the page and the README quote the file's own numbers and years.
- The at-scale check (ADR 0108) is not widened (2026 to 2030); its `all_checks` total is recomputed from the cached answers so it
  counts the new later-year pairs.

## Result of the run (15 November 2026, 22:36 MYT, real JPL Horizons API)

- 60 pairs, 51 of them in 2031 to 2040, every year 2028 to 2040 present. Same solar power answer 60 of 60, same Earth answer
  60 of 60. Every elevation difference at most 0.0137°.
- Largest difference 5.4775°: the Sun azimuth at Apollo 11, 2028-02-08 14:14 UT, with the Sun 0.56° from the zenith. The engine at
  that exact minute is 0.0006° from Horizons in elevation and 0.0014° on the sky; the error comes from the page joining hourly rows
  with straight lines in azimuth and elevation, which near the zenith moves the shown Sun 0.056° on the sky. The same happens, much
  smaller, with the Sun 85° to 88° below the horizon (0.084° in azimuth, 2036 and 2037).
- The rule is not met, so `passed` is false and the cards say the dates after the precomputed tables are not validated. The
  first-screen total (`all_checks`, 900 pairs) now shows a largest difference of 5.4775°.

## Second run: directions joined between rows (15 November 2026, 23:30 to 00:00 MYT)

- `sampleAt` (`src/ephem.js`, used for every value the page shows, frozen tables and engine alike) now joins the unit direction
  vectors (east, north, up) of two hourly rows with a straight line, normalises the result and turns it back into azimuth and
  elevation (`lerpDirection`). Rows stay exact. Near the horizon it agrees with the old straight lines far below the 0.01° rounding of
  the tables; near the zenith it keeps the body on its arc. The competing entry that covers 2027 to 2040 does the same with vectors.
- Every JPL Horizons check was run again with the same seeds and the same rule (`scripts/validate.py`: table and engine pairs, fresh
  requests; `scripts/validate_years.py`: 60 pairs, fresh requests; `scripts/validate.py --scale`: cached answers, shown values
  recomputed). Each row now also carries `sky_deg`, the great-circle angle between the shown and the Horizons direction, for
  information; the Validation page gives its largest value for the later years. The pass rules are unchanged.
- Later years: largest difference 0.1465° (was 5.4775°), the same Sun azimuth at Apollo 11 with the Sun 0.56° from the zenith; on the
  sky that pair is 0.0015° (was 0.056°). Largest on the sky over all 60 pairs: 0.0047° Sun, 0.0035° Earth. Same answers 60 of 60.
- **The rule is still not met at that one pair.** What is left is not the interpolation: the engine itself at that exact minute is
  0.137° from Horizons in azimuth, 0.0014° on the sky, and near the zenith the azimuth multiplies a direction difference by about
  1 / cos(elevation) ≈ 100. A cubic interpolation between rows cannot remove it. Meeting the 0.05° azimuth rule there would need the
  engine to agree with Horizons to about 0.0005° on the sky, or the author to decide to judge directions on the sky (great circle)
  instead of azimuth near the zenith. Neither is done here; the cards keep saying the later dates are not validated.

## Third step: the rule judges directions on the sky (author's decision, 15 November 2026, 23:51 MYT)

- The author decided that the later-year check is judged on the directions on the sky, as the competing entry reports its own check
  ("Sun and Earth directions against NASA/JPL Horizons"; all of its sites are polar, so it never meets a Sun near the zenith).
  `config/years.json` `check.max_sky_deg` = 0.05: at every pair the Sun and the Earth direction shown are within 0.05° of Horizons on a
  great circle, and every yes/no answer is the same. `max_diff_deg` is gone; `src/years.js` `checkPassed` and
  `scripts/validate_years.py` `passed` use the new rule, and the tests say a large azimuth difference near the zenith does not decide.
- Azimuth and elevation differences are still reported in full. The Validation page says that azimuth is undefined near the zenith
  and names the pair with the largest azimuth difference (site and distance of the Sun from the zenith, read from the file).
- Results regenerated from the answers already recorded, with no new Horizons request: `scripts/validate_years.py --reuse` (the 60
  recorded answers) and `scripts/validate.py --scale` (cache; 0 requests sent). Later years: on the sky 0.0047° for the Sun and
  0.0035° for Earth, same answers 60 of 60, so `passed` is true and the cards say the engine is validated against JPL Horizons for
  the dates after the precomputed tables, to the end of 2040. Largest azimuth difference still 0.1465° (Apollo 11, Sun 0.56° from the
  zenith).
- The first-screen line now gives the directions on the sky over every check together: 900 pairs, largest difference 0.0063°
  (`all_checks.sky_separation`), same answers 900 of 900.

## Rollback

Set `stop`, `check.to` and `check.pairs` back (2031-01-01, 24), `max_sky_deg` back to `max_diff_deg` 0.05 (with the old
`checkPassed`/`passed`), run `python3 scripts/validate_years.py` and then
`python3 scripts/validate.py --scale`, and revert the text and test changes of this record.

## Fourth step: dates to the end of 2050 (16 November 2026, 00:08 to 00:22 MYT, branch wt/tahun2050)

- `config/years.json` `stop` = 2051-01-01 00:00 (all of 31 December 2050). No code change for the range.
- The check keeps its 60 pairs in 2028 to 2040 (seed 20261116, the Horizons answers already recorded) and gets one more block,
  `check.extra`: 24 random pairs in 2041 to 2050 with their own fixed seed (20261118), each asked fresh from the JPL Horizons API with
  the script's pause between requests. `scripts/validate_years.py --reuse` asks only for pairs the file does not hold yet.
- Same rule (directions on the sky within 0.05° for the Sun and Earth, same answers). Result: 84 pairs, on the sky 0.0055° for the Sun
  and 0.0035° for Earth, same answers 84 of 84, so the cards say validated to the end of 2050. Largest azimuth difference still 0.1465°
  (Apollo 11, Sun 0.56° from the zenith). Every check together: 924 pairs, same answers 924 of 924, on the sky at most 0.0063°.
- The summary's angle on the sky is now the largest of the rows' own `sky_deg` (from the unrounded shown direction), so the two
  always agree; before, the summary used the shown values rounded to 4 decimals (0.0054° against 0.0055° here).
